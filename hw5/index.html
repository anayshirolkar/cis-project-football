Should the U.S. Raise the Federal Minimum Wage?

Anay Shirolkar · abshirol@asu.edu

The Topic

Increasing the current federal minimum wage of $7.25 remains the central issue in American economic policy. Those who favor an increase in the minimum wage say that inflation has continually reduced its purchasing power and that raising it increases low-income workers' salaries, while those against believe that one-size-fits-all approach poses a risk of reducing employment opportunities in areas where cost of living is low and also forgets about the 30 states and Washington D.C., which have set higher wages on their own. The graphs below illustrate how the same set of data from the Department of Labor (as of January 1, 2020), can tell conflicting stories.

Dataset: US Minimum Wage by State from 1968 to 2020 (Joe Lisle, Kaggle; compiled from the U.S. Department of Labor).

Left Chart: Rhetorical Techniques

The use of such a graph in making a case for raising the minimum wage is based on inflation adjustment to 2020 dollars and setting the reference point at the peak value in 1979 ($10.33), thus shifting the horizontal line of equal purchasing power to a dramatic decrease of 30%. Limiting the vertical scale from 0 to $6 highlights the magnitude of that decrease by making the shaded portion of "lost buying power" visually predominant. Red color and related associations are used as visual prompts, and the title and "FROZEN AT $7.25" banner make the reader understand that Congress did not keep the wages unchanged but actively cut them. Expressing the difference between 1979 and 2020 in annual rather than hourly terms makes the argument more relevant: "$6,406 less a year for a full-time worker".

Right Chart: Rhetorical Techniques

The graph tries to dissuade government mandate because it takes into account only a one-time snapshot:nominal effective minimum wages as of January 1, 2020, with no consideration for the effects of several decades of inflation. Setting zero point on the x-axis allows the bars to be proportional in terms of their size and makes $7.25 look like an unchangeable norm. Arranging all 50 states and the District of Columbia in descending order creates two groups of states: a green (showing progress and initiative) majority and a grey (indifference) minority. The title of the chart ("28 states + D.C. have already raised their minimum wage on their own"), the title ("Without Washington"), and the phrase "their voters' choice to make" imply that a federal mandate is redundant and that maintaining the status quo is democracy in action.
